# Round 1 — independent educational review

I am an AI reviewer applying instructional criteria, not a proven elite human educator or a sampled novice. I inspected the raw 64-slide `course-v2.json`, learner-facing branches in `v/lesson/src/PcaScenes.tsx`, `PcaDerivationVisuals.tsx`, and all four requested screenshots before reading any prior assessment. I did not run a learner study or interact with the live app. “Failed inference” below means a specific inference the artifact inadequately supports, not an observed failure by every learner.

## Primary diagnosis

The revision makes individual operations inspectable but does not consistently make their necessity emerge from a learner-owned question. It seems optimized around decomposing an expert derivation into many visible steps. That is different from establishing a reason to care, anticipating what the learner currently believes, and creating a sequence in which the next mathematical idea feels needed. Technical correctness and 64 screens cannot establish those outcomes.

### 1. The learner is given a procedure before a purpose

Slide 1 asks “Can two coordinates become one?” and the rendered opening says to turn an axis, keep one score, and watch error change (`PcaScenes.tsx:1042–1051`). This states the operation clearly. It does not establish why this learner would willingly replace accurate measurements with inaccurate ones, what practical problem makes fewer coordinates valuable, or what they will be able to understand or do afterward. “Four markers on a flat board” supplies units and a scene, but not stakes or a consequential decision.

The unsupported inference is: “Reducing coordinates is worth doing, and closeness after reconstruction is the right success criterion for my purpose.” That criterion is selected for the learner. Uses and limitations arrive in slides 57–60, after the proof. A learner can comply through dozens of operations while still asking why PCA is useful. A general opening requirement should therefore ask for a concrete need, a payoff, and a test of that payoff; it should not merely mandate an introductory paragraph titled “Why.”

### 2. Naming the bridge is repeatedly mistaken for crossing it

Slides 13–15 move from a direction arrow to `cos θ, sin θ`, a dot product, and a matrix product. Slide 14 says a dot product gives the signed coordinate; `PcaScenes.tsx:1231–1242` displays the two multiplications and their sum. That supports calculating the number. It does not clearly develop why weighted original coordinates equal distance along the tilted ruler. The learner must supply the key geometric meaning of the dot product.

Slide 7 asserts that maximum kept spread and minimum error agree, with the promised Pythagorean explanation deferred to slides 51–52. Previewing a result is legitimate, but the course spends most of its central development optimizing variance after motivating reconstruction error. The learner is asked to trust their equivalence for roughly forty screens. The missing causal bridge is more consequential than another algebra expansion.

Slide 32 introduces a unit tangent, a gradient, a directional derivative, first-order change, and second-order change. The next slide derives the gradient and slide 34 explains the constraint normal. The rendered stationarity screenshot simultaneously shows direction space, a separately scaled gradient decomposition, and `dV/dθ = 2tᵀSw`; the learner must connect all three. The “Why this step” sentence explains what the tangent dot product does, but not why. `PcaScenes.tsx:1430–1454` reuses the full stationarity lab across all four slides. The model is exposed before its ingredients have been established.

Slide 37 compresses “nonzero vector maps to zero → singular → determinant zero” into a brief explanation. Slide 56 introduces SVD and its orthogonality cancellation in one slide. These may be reasonable for a mathematically prepared learner; they are insufficiently justified as prerequisites for an interested 20-year-old whose preparation has not been established. Age alone does not define prior knowledge.

### 3. Many controls expose an answer without eliciting an inference

The covariance lab is a substantial improvement: selected features, row/column correspondence, individual products, accumulation, and division are visible together (`PcaDerivationVisuals.tsx:94–265`; repaired covariance screenshot). However, pressing “Add A’s product” and then advancing through the sum can produce completion without deciding which values to multiply or explaining why the columns belong together. The controls execute the reasoning rather than necessarily testing it.

Similarly, the stationarity lab offers “Largest-variance direction” and “Other stationary direction” buttons (`PcaDerivationVisuals.tsx:435–442`). These are useful inspection shortcuts, but weaken a task framed as finding the stationary directions. Its prediction is generated by the software; the learner need not predict a turn’s effect first. This does not make the interaction worthless. It means control activation is not evidence of conceptual activity.

Most early tasks say turn, compare, or follow. Numerical production appears at slide 63; the fresh-cloud challenge at slide 62. Those are valuable but late. The check explicitly says written reasons need separate review (`PcaScenes.tsx:777–828,1836–1952`). That honesty should be retained, while recognizing that completion provides no evidence that the intended explanation was learned.

### 4. Added detail can compete with the explanation

At the captured 1280×800 opening, the recovery card continues below the viewport and the focused “TRY IT” instruction is below the visible portion. At stationarity, the small-turn controls and numerical prediction/comparison are likewise below the screenshot’s visible region. These are not proof of universal layout failure; they are concrete evidence that the intended explanatory unit is not visible as a unit in these captures.

The original `pca-preview.png` has fewer controls and a compact one-screen presentation, but begins its displayed slide with several projection formulas and terms such as loading and residual. The repair reduces some early symbolic burden and clarifies units, yet expands the learner’s reading, scrolling, and coordination burden. More fine-grained content is not automatically better pacing.

## Strengths to retain

The stable four-point example makes arithmetic tractable and supports continuity. Projection loss, coordinate rotation, score versus direction, and reconstruction are represented concretely. Covariance signs are contrasted with unchanged feature variances (slides 24–25). The maximum proof uses nonnegative squared weights and explicitly distinguishes candidate stationarity from a global maximum. Equal eigenvalues, units, outliers, and low-variance useful information prevent simplistic PCA claims. Numerical checks and the teach-back avoid falsely certifying conceptual mastery. These are assets for a better teaching route, not evidence that the current route has succeeded.

## Alternative causal diagnosis and falsification

An alternative is that the central issue is guidance and medium, not missing explanatory content. A skilled live narrator could sequence attention across the existing diagrams, elicit predictions, pause before symbols, and connect each operation to a persistent question. A self-paced interface leaves that temporal orchestration to the novice. The repeated rejection could also reflect a mismatch with this particular user’s taste or desired depth, rather than generally ineffective teaching. I cannot infer universal educational failure from rejection alone or establish parity with expert videos without matched comparison.

Test these alternatives with target learners, comparable prior knowledge, and equal time: current self-paced lesson, the same content with a concise guided narration, and a relevant expert explanation. Ask learners to predict a new cloud’s useful axis, explain why projection gives its score, connect spread to error, and distinguish stationary candidates from maxima. Use a delayed new-example explanation to test durability. If unaided learners already transfer these inferences, my bridge diagnosis weakens. If narration alone markedly improves transfer, temporal guidance is a stronger cause than content omission. If outcomes are comparable but preference differs, report a preference mismatch rather than pedagogical superiority.

## Three generalizable requirements

1. **Purpose before machinery:** specify the learner’s meaningful problem, promised capability, and observable success; test whether the opening communicates them before introducing implementation vocabulary.
2. **Audit inferences, not coverage:** for each essential transition, record prior belief, new question, explanatory bridge, likely misconception, and a transfer prompt. A named concept or correct formula does not satisfy the bridge.
3. **Require evidence appropriate to the claim:** an interaction must have an intended prediction or inference and a way to inspect the learner’s reasoning. Separate artifact correctness, usability, immediate learning, delayed transfer, and preference; no aggregate quality score should substitute for these.

## Three tempting overfits to avoid

1. Mandating a particular story, animation, slide count, or “interactivity on every slide.” The function is motivation and inference support; the form should vary by topic.
2. Removing rigorous mathematics because this derivation overloads some transitions. Rigour can be essential; preparation and sequencing determine accessibility.
3. Treating one dissatisfied user, one AI panel, or one polished reference as a universal novice model. Establish the actual audience and use disconfirming learner evidence.
