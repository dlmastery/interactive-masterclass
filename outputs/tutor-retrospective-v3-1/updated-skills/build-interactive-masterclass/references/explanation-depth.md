# Explain the same idea at different depths

For this user's flagship courses, understanding should support explanation to a 10-year-old, a 15-year-old, an undergraduate, a graduate student, a PhD researcher, and a postdoc. The objective is the learner's flexible grasp of the subject, not merely six scripts written by an agent. Use this reference at planning, critical-bridge design, and review. Brief answers do not trigger six full lessons.

## One core, several entry points

Write the core relation in ordinary language: the question, relevant objects, mechanism or warrant, result, and essential boundary. Choose a concrete case that makes the relation visible. Identify what the learner must be able to predict, explain, or decide. Use that core to connect representations; simpler and more formal accounts should not contradict each other.

Age and qualifications are shorthand for possible preparation, not fixed limits or a ranking of intelligence. A postdoc outside the field may need an elementary entry; a younger learner may be ready for formal reasoning. Diagnose actual familiarity. Avoid childish tone, invented age-based reading rules, and the assumption that an advanced learner prefers jargon. Experts still need purpose and intuition.

## The six requested lenses

Adapt these working expectations to the topic and learner. They guide depth, not a compulsory six-chapter sequence.

| Lens | Explanatory burden | Evidence of understanding to seek |
|---|---|---|
| Around age 10: concrete intuition | A familiar or directly observable situation, a real question, a visible relation, a useful result, and one honest boundary. Use physical, geometric, narrative, or sensory reasoning as appropriate. | Learner explains or draws what happens, predicts a changed case, and says when the simple rule may fail. |
| Around age 15: connected model | Explain the mechanism with quantities, graphs, simple relationships, or structured evidence when appropriate. Ground each new abstraction in the concrete account. | Learner connects representations, reasons through a small example, and explains the difference between two plausible answers. |
| Undergraduate: operational and justified understanding | Define the objects, derive or justify the central method, work a complete case, and interpret assumptions, results, applications, and alternatives. | Learner reconstructs the main argument, performs or interprets the method, and explains why it serves the original goal. |
| Graduate: formal depth and generalization | Expose conditions of validity, proof structure or methodological warrant, important edge cases, generalizations, and nontrivial tradeoffs. | Learner identifies which assumption a claim needs, builds or interprets a counterexample, and justifies a choice between approaches. |
| PhD: research judgment | Evaluate competing formulations and evidence, distinguish established results from live questions, and interpret the strength and limits of research claims. | Learner critiques a specific claim using its evidence, identifies what is still unknown, and proposes a discriminating test or argument. |
| Postdoc: synthesis and research design | Connect the idea to neighboring approaches, compare their explanatory power, and reason about what evidence could justify an extension or change in practice. Retain access to the simple core. | Learner defends and revises a synthesis under objections, designs a plausible investigation, and explains its stakes in plain language. Novel publishable discovery is not a prerequisite for a good explanation. |

These are proposed design expectations, not validated developmental stages. In history, rigor may mean source criticism and interpretation; in craft, perceptual discrimination and performance; in mathematics, definitions and proof. Do not force equations or a research frontier onto a subject where they do not serve the promise.

## Make the bridges visible

For a central concept, keep one compact depth map with: shared concept ID; the learning person's demonstrated preparation; the intended explanation recipient's assumed knowledge; explanation and representation at each relevant lens; what remains true; what is omitted, qualified, or added; the question that needs the next depth; and a teach-back task. These are two different audience contracts: an undergraduate may be learning to explain to a child. Diagnose a knowledge gap in the learner separately from poor adaptation to the recipient. Cover all six lenses in the flagship plan, marking unauthored or untested ones honestly. This does not require building six entire courses when only one route is authorized.

An upward bridge starts with something the current account cannot yet answer. Add only the needed definition, precision, assumption, technique, or evidence; show how it answers that question. Introduce prerequisite math or context here, then return to the subject. An advanced derivation must explain the original visible effect, not become an unrelated exercise.

A downward bridge translates the formal account back into objects, actions, and consequences. Explain why each important quantity or evidential distinction exists. Removing technical words without restoring their meaning is not simplification. One running case can provide continuity; use additional cases when needed to expose limits or transfer, rather than stretching an analogy beyond its useful range.

Track simplifications explicitly: claim, valid scope, omitted detail, likely false inference, and later refinement. Some models are approximations that must be replaced outside their domain. Say so at the accessible level in suitable language; do not plant a false universal rule that a later lesson quietly retracts. A picture can supply intuition without pretending to prove the theorem.

## Teach the learner to make the explanation

Model an explanation and expose the choices behind it. Then ask the learner to explain the idea to a specified audience using a fresh example, draw or operate a representation, and answer a natural follow-up. Reduce prompts over successive attempts. Offer written, spoken, visual, or supported formats; unfamiliarity with public speaking is distinct from a conceptual gap.

Check meaning, not resemblance to the model answer:

- Does the explanation retain the core relation, purpose, and essential boundary?
- Does it use only prerequisites established for that audience, defining needed terms as they arise?
- Can the learner answer a why/how question and predict a changed case without reciting the script?
- Can they move upward into the relevant justification, then back into ordinary language without changing the claim?
- Can they recognize where their analogy stops working and choose a better representation?

Feedback identifies the specific lost relation, unsupported jump, false implication, or missing evidence. Repair that bridge, then use a different case. A correct explanation after heavy assistance is a supported attempt; verify independently before claiming unaided ability. Research-level explanation requires relevant evidence and preparation: a short course must not imply it has granted field expertise.

## Review and routing

Compare adjacent accounts for contradiction and hidden prerequisites. A domain reviewer checks fidelity; a reviewer restricted to the stated entry knowledge checks accessibility and continuity. Record their actual knowledge and shared context. AI role-play is a useful inspection, not proof that a child or expert learned from the course.

Test simple-to-formal and formal-to-simple translation, an analogy failure, a changed example, and audience-specific follow-ups on the central concepts. Assess depth-specific tasks separately rather than averaging a strong expert answer with an inaccurate accessible story. Record actual learner attempts only when observed; authored prompts and reviewer answers are not learner outcomes.

Give learners clear entry routes and optional depth controls, with an explanation of what an advanced branch adds. Preserve their place after a refresher. The course should make deeper understanding reachable while respecting the selected audience and scope; length and degree labels are not evidence of depth.
