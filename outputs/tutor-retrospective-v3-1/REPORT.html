1. The learner must be able to make the explanation

The important change is from adapting the author's wording to developing the learner's ability to reconstruct and adapt the idea. Six polished scripts are insufficient. A learner should be able to preserve the core relationship, choose an appropriate example, answer a follow-up, and say where the account stops applying.

Requested lensWhat the explanation must make possible
About age 10A concrete, accurate intuition: explain what happens, draw or show the relationship, predict a changed case, and recognize a simple boundary.
About age 15A connected model using suitable quantities, graphs, or structured evidence, with each abstraction tied back to the concrete case.
UndergraduateThe central method and its justification, a complete worked example, assumptions, interpretation, and a reasoned choice of application.
GraduateConditions of validity, formal or methodological depth, counterexamples, generalizations, and significant tradeoffs.
PhDCritique of specific research claims and competing formulations, with evidence, uncertainties, and discriminating questions.
PostdocSynthesis across approaches, defensible research decisions, and plain-language explanation of why an investigation matters. A good explanation does not require a novel publishable discovery.

These are working design lenses, not validated developmental stages or measures of intelligence. A postdoc outside a field may need a concrete introduction. A younger learner may be prepared for more mathematics. The course routes by demonstrated preparation and purpose.

The learning person's knowledge and the explanation recipient's knowledge are now separate fields. An undergraduate learning to explain density to a child needs sufficient physics knowledge themselves, and must also translate it for the child's entry knowledge. A correct formula can fail the second requirement; an accessible but false story fails the first.

The new depth reference specifies a shared conceptual core, explicit bridges into rigor, explanations back in ordinary language, simplification boundaries, fresh examples, and audience-specific teach-back. A full flagship plan maps all six lenses; the authorized course scope determines which full routes are built. An age label must not force every learner through six courses.

3. What Welch Labs adds specifically

The research traced actual written progression in Neural Networks Demystified and official material for Imaginary Numbers Are Real. It did not infer pedagogy merely from episode titles.

  1. Keep a case alive as understanding grows. Retain the meaning of inputs, outputs, symbols, and operations while stronger machinery is added.
  2. Make an obstacle earn the next method. The learner should be able to state what the current approach cannot do and what the proposed change needs to resolve.
  3. Connect representations through learner work. Ask the learner to compare examples and form a conjecture, then supply the relevant justification and a new test. Numerical cases alone are not a proof.
  4. Teach verification before trusting a result. Explain why a different checking route can reveal an error that plausible output would conceal.
  5. Challenge apparent success. Use a successful demonstration as the starting point for validity, failure diagnosis, and mitigation.
  6. Keep a familiar visual anchor during a change of view. Make the new representation's scope visible, including what it leaves out.

These are adaptations from the official neural-network sequence, the creator's notebooks, and the complex-plane assignment with its multiplication follow-up. The Welch research note records exact notebook cells, source versions, limitations, and technical statements that must not be copied uncritically.

Admiration is a reason to inspect a source carefully. It is not an exemption from mathematical or software verification. Useful teaching structures can be retained while correcting source defects, modernizing technical practice, or improving the visibility of qualifications.

4. What changes in preparation, execution, and review

During strategy selection: compare candidate routes using their opening need, pivotal conceptual change, running case, practical decision, and closing payoff. Map audience depths onto shared concept IDs. Select compatible techniques that serve those demands.

During scene construction: identify the learner's current model, intended inference, representation, evidence, and expected reasoning. Specify what an abstraction adds and what remains valid from the simpler account. A narrative transition must have an explanatory reason.

During tutoring: collect the learner's reasoning, distinguish a conceptual gap from poor audience adaptation, provide targeted help, and use a fresh case after repair. Direct explanation remains appropriate. A scene need not demand a click, prediction, or answer merely to count as interactive.

During review: check truth in both directions, simple-to-formal and formal-to-simple. Check source claims, visual/spoken agreement, the opening payoff, analogy limits, and independent reconstruction. Use the requested recipient's knowledge contract rather than age stereotypes.

During production: retain exact reviewed notes and cues; choose a scripted, conversational, or hybrid delivery that preserves meaning. Actual delivery needs actual listening and viewing. The added patterns do not create a fixed episode count, duration, camera style, or extra set of mandatory documents.
