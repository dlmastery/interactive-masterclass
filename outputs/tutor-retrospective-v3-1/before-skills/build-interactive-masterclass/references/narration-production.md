# Narration, illustration, and publication-quality delivery

Read for an explainer intended for presentation or video. Speaker notes are part of the teaching design, not an afterthought. The goal is an accurate, memorable, coherent explanation a presenter can actually deliver.

## Plan the spoken promise

Specify the target listener, initial question, promised capability, difficult inferences, applications, limits, and closing payoff. Design chapters around questions resolved, not equal slide counts. A course may exceed 100 slides; the relevant test is whether each scene helps and the whole route remains usable.

Develop exact narration with the visual sequence. Use natural speech, precise referents, and causal transitions. Explain why the next operation is useful. Avoid reciting slide bullets, repeated promises, hype, “obviously,” and formulas without meaning. A surprising opening must be truthful and pay off in the lesson.

Do not imitate a named creator's voice or copy their script. Study techniques and create an original argument, example set, visual grammar, and speaker identity.

## Event-cued speaker notes

For each meaningful scene/cue include:

| Field | What a second presenter needs |
|---|---|
| Scene/cue ID and learning job | The inference or decision served |
| Exact spoken text | Natural sentences, with pronunciation help for unfamiliar names/symbols when needed |
| Visual before/after | Objects, labels, values, annotations, and retained reference state |
| Action and pointer | Named target, reveal, transformation, control movement, or annotation |
| Pause/pace/emphasis | Where to let the learner inspect or think, and what deserves emphasis |
| Learner prompt and response | Prediction or task, plausible responses, feedback, help/re-entry cues |
| Transition | Why the next scene follows and what remains unresolved |
| Evidence and boundary | Source/credit, assumptions, and limits relevant to the spoken claim |
| Timing | Estimated initially; measured after actual rehearsal |

Separate internal production cues from spoken language. “Point there” or “explain covariance” is not a usable note. A presenter should know exactly what to say, show, point at, wait for, and do next. Keep notes connected to the current selected state in an interactive version.

Example cue structure, not a reviewed lesson:
- SAY: “These two routes share the same original report. If that report is wrong, both copies can repeat its error.”
- SHOW: one source node feeding two branches; preserve the original claim text.
- POINT: original source node, then both branches.
- PAUSE: allow the viewer to trace the shared origin.
- ASK: “Would a third copy settle the claim?”
- FOLLOW: explain why a source with independent event evidence changes the situation.

## Speak mathematics and technical reasoning

State what an expression measures before reading it. Map symbols to the actual objects, units, dimensions, indices, and assumptions. Explain why a term, denominator, sign, constraint, or operation appears. Preserve the previous line when comparison carries the argument.

Speak the reasoning, not every glyph: name a sum's population, a derivative's change, a constraint's purpose, or a matrix product's pairing. Define names consistently across audio, notation, diagrams, and feedback. Distinguish definition, numerical illustration, general identity, approximation, necessary condition, and sufficient/global proof.

Use pauses at genuine inferential demands. Do not prescribe one words-per-minute rate for a familiar recap and a new proof. A pause or extra slide should give the viewer something specific to inspect or reconstruct.

When a demonstration can be explained by a plausible competing cause, include a cue that distinguishes it: state what changed, what stayed comparable, and why the observation supports this conclusion rather than the rival. Do not leave the listener to invent that bridge. Reviewers must tie a demanded contrast to the public learner objective; a hidden rubric's extra preference is not automatically a defect in the lesson. This applies to confounding factors, alternative interpretations, and misleading perceptual comparisons without imposing the same example on every topic.

## Produce an animatic before expensive polish

Combine rough accurate visuals with the intended narration. Rehearse the opening, hardest bridge, application/limit decision, and transfer first; then rehearse the complete route. Read aloud or generate and actually listen to the intended audio when tools permit. A written script review cannot be relabeled as listened-to delivery.

Record actual stumbling, unclear referents, breath/phrase problems, pronunciation, rushed inferences, premature answers, dead time, and competing reading/listening demands. Estimated duration remains an estimate until rehearsal. Revise the script, timing, or representation based on observed problems. Maintain measured timings and resolved findings with the artifact revision.

Produce final illustrations after the core argument and rough sequence work. Use precise code-native diagrams for exact geometry, data, labels, and transformations when appropriate; use other original/licensed imagery where it serves the explanation. A style preference does not excuse misleading structure.

## Review through distinct channels

1. **Silent-picture review:** trace objects, changes, comparisons, scale, labels, and visual questions in the rendered sequence. Not every argument must be recoverable without audio; the picture must support rather than contradict it.
2. **Audio-only review:** listen for purpose, logical sequence, reference clarity, vocabulary, and transitions. Identify essential visual information needing description or an accessible alternative.
3. **Combined timed review:** play actual audio and visuals together. Check pointer/reveal alignment, time to inspect, caption correctness, pronunciation, diagram/formula agreement, and attention overload.
4. **Cold-presenter handoff:** have another presenter use the notes and operational cues without author coaching where feasible. If only an AI reads the notes, label that narrower inspection.
5. **Learner reasoning review:** obtain uncoached prediction, explanation, use/no-use judgment, failure diagnosis, and transfer. Record help. Knowledgeable AI answers are not evidence of novice learning.
6. **Whole-course review:** verify the opening promise, complete argument, breadth, transitions, and ending payoff after local repairs.

Unperformed tests stay unperformed. If audio tools are unavailable, complete script/storyboard review and state the oral-test gap. Do not claim oration is perfected from a word count or screenshot.

## Two delivery paths

**Linear video:** author a deliberate demonstration path, chosen inputs, contrasts, failure cases, pause-and-predict moments, and explained answers. It cannot react to a viewer's unobserved response. Include useful chapters and a companion practice route where commissioned.

**Interactive tutor/slides:** couple narration and feedback to actual state. A changed input must update dependent words, figures, formulas, and verdicts. Student interruption stops stale speech/pointing. Save the pending task and return point. Test authored branches, detours, back/forward, reset, and unsupported responses. Never play unconditional “correct” feedback.

Share the conceptual route and source assets; do not assume a notebook export is an edited video or that a video script is an adaptive tutor.

## Accessibility and delivery package

Plan readable text/equations, contrast, non-color distinctions, purposeful motion, keyboard access for interactions, clear audio, relevant visual description, captions, and transcript. Inspect the actual target viewport; a large authoring monitor is not sufficient. See [W3C audio/video content guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/av-content/) and [integrated descriptions](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Techniques/general/G226). These are design inputs, not proof of completed accessibility compliance.

Deliver the requested editable deck/notebook, event-cued notes, script, storyboard, illustration sources, credits/licenses, launch or demonstration path, and review evidence. Include actual audio/video and corrected captions when commissioned and produced. Verify exported speaker notes, links, figure proportions, and chapter/navigation behavior. Keep delivered-file checks separate from source-file checks.

## Public success and the post-publication loop

Quality, demonstrated learning, and distribution are separate outcomes. [YouTube's recommendation guidance](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16533387?hl=en) describes audience relevance and long-term satisfaction; it provides no guaranteed path to a view count.

Use truthful titles/thumbnails that communicate the audience, puzzle, and payoff. Avoid a more dramatic promise than the lesson fulfills. Search demand, topic size, existing audience, traffic source, competing material, language, and timing affect reach. Do not guarantee millions of views or a date for top ratings.

After authorized publication, inspect appropriate metrics and qualitative feedback:
- [Retention](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415?hl=en): link dips, spikes, and strong moments to scene IDs; replay can indicate confusion as well as interest.
- [CTR and impressions](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/7628154?hl=en): interpret by traffic/audience context, not as a universal quality threshold.
- [Title/thumbnail experiments](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16391400?hl=en): use platform-supported tests for honest packaging; their outcome is not a learning measure.
- Comments and questions: treat as leads to investigate, not representative proof of mastery.

Form a specific hypothesis, revise the relevant scene or packaging, compare suitable evidence, and retain corrections. Never remove a necessary justification solely to improve watch metrics. Publication, paid promotion, and scheduled follow-up require actual authorization; preparing this plan does not perform those actions.
