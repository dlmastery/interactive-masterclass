# Creator research: 3Blue1Brown and Veritasium

This is a focused process comparison, researched 7 October 2026 UTC, with prior shared authoring context. It is not a ranking of the “best” creators or a 30-video topic survey. I inspected written creator guidance, published research, and thesis text; I did **not** watch or hear any videos. The additions below are proposed implementations, not claims that the creators use this exact checklist or that our skill has demonstrated their results.

1. **Open a consequential problem, then return to it with the new model.** Sanderson recommends examples before frameworks and putting the key exercise near the beginning. Add an opening prediction or achievable subproblem that reveals what the later abstraction buys; revisit it after the explanation. Useful when formal definitions otherwise seem arbitrary. Avoid turning his strong advice against opening definitions into an absolute ban: essential vocabulary or safety context may come first, and novices need enough support to attempt the problem. [Creator guidance, FAQ on making math videos](https://www.3blue1brown.com/about/).

2. **Audit each visual change against the spoken inference.** Sanderson asks for deliberate movement and visuals reinforcing the narration. For each difficult scene, name what the learner must notice and how the visual change supports that sentence; retain a static comparison when it helps. Useful for transformations, accumulation, geometry, or changing relationships. Misuse is cosmetic equation motion, concurrent competing messages, or assuming Manim itself supplies explanation. The proposed audit is our operationalization of his advice. [Same creator guidance, visualization/animation bullets](https://www.3blue1brown.com/about/).

3. **Show why a plausible wrong model fails, then repair it.** Muller and colleagues randomly assigned 364 first-year physics students to four multimedia treatments. Explicit refutation and student–tutor dialogue produced larger conceptual gains than exposition. Add a brief prediction with reasoning, a charitable competing explanation, discriminating evidence, and an explicit replacement model. Use when a consequential misconception is plausible or observed. Do not manufacture confusion for every topic, ridicule a learner, or merely announce that an answer is wrong. A modeled dialogue must be labeled as such; it is not evidence of actual learner behavior. This study supports misconception discussion in its mechanics setting, not universal superiority of dialogue or artificial confusion. [Published study abstract: *Saying the wrong thing*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1365-2729.2007.00248.x).

4. **Check reconstruction before trusting confidence or apparent clarity.** Muller's thesis reports that learners could feel they had learned while retaining alternative conceptions; its limitations call for stronger probing than multiple-choice answers alone. Add a short explanation, drawing, or changed-case prediction after the pivotal passage. Optionally ask confidence when distinguishing a guess from a committed wrong model would alter feedback. Compare the reasoning with the actual explanation. Useful when polished delivery makes understanding feel effortless. Do not grade low confidence as ignorance or high confidence as mastery. The thesis studied particular short physics interventions and explicitly leaves longer-term generalization open. [Thesis text, §§9.4.3, 11.2 and 11.4](https://studylib.net/doc/18658765/designing-effective-multimedia-for-physics-education).

5. **Provide a deliberate transition from watching to doing.** Sanderson's learning recommendations prioritize practice over continued passive consumption and praise problems embedded in progression. After an explanatory payoff, give the learner something to reconstruct, vary, diagnose, or explain to someone else, with useful feedback and reduced assistance on a subsequent attempt. This turns admiration of an explanation into a chance to acquire its reasoning. Avoid compulsory trivial clicks or an endless queue of videos. This is creator advice; the particular practice design and teach-back extension are our proposals. [*Recommendations*, “Practice”](https://www.3blue1brown.com/blog/recommendations/).

6. **Preserve purposeful interest; test detours rather than deleting them mechanically.** Muller, Lee and Sharma compared concise and extended stellar-spectra lessons with 104 school/university learners. The extended version added interesting related astronomy; measured performance was similar. This does not establish a learning advantage from extra material. It does caution against assuming every nonessential detail necessarily harms learning. A proposed skill move is to keep a surprising application or human question when it gives the learner a reason to investigate, then reconnect it explicitly to the mechanism. Remove or relocate it if it obscures the route. Avoid treating entertainment, longer viewing, or a nonsignificant result as proof of deeper understanding. [Published study abstract: *Coherence or interest?*](https://ajet.org.au/index.php/AJET/article/view/1223).

The skill already contains motivation, practice, meaningful visuals, and misconception checks. These should become more concrete scene-level moves and review questions, not six additional mandatory documents. Sanderson also warns against copying pacing across teaching, journalism, and learner-discovery genres; choose the approach for this learner's purpose. [Creator guidance, genre bullet](https://www.3blue1brown.com/about/).
