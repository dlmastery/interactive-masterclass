# Complete subject coverage without a disconnected encyclopedia

Read at intake and before final course acceptance. The question is what the learner can explain and decide, not whether a heading exists. A flagship explainer needs breadth, depth, natural progression, and an appropriate route through them.

## Coverage map

For each dimension below record: the topic-specific learner question; a scene/problem ID; the explanatory witness or evidence; a check when useful; source/date where needed; and status (planned, authored, reviewed, verified, unverified, not applicable). “Not applicable” needs a subject-specific reason. Do not silently omit a dimension or mark a title as coverage.

| Dimension | Learner question and required substance |
|---|---|
| Purpose and gain | Why learn this? What can I explain, decide, build, or notice afterward? State the promise and fulfill it. |
| What it is and is not | What problem/idea/method is this? Define scope, neighboring concepts, and a common mistaken identity. |
| Why it exists | What inadequacy, puzzle, constraint, or historical need made it useful? Explain why the chosen goal/criterion addresses that need. |
| When to use it | Which conditions make it appropriate? What signs should trigger consideration? Include a use/no-use decision. |
| Where it fits | Where does it sit in a larger system, workflow, discipline, or argument? Identify inputs, outputs, upstream/downstream consequences. |
| Mechanism and intuition | What changes, what remains, and what causes or warrants the result? Use a representation the learner can reconstruct. |
| Foundations at use | What background unlocks the next inference? Supply it at the point of need and return to the main question. |
| Derivation or justification | Why does it work? Preserve requested mathematical proof, empirical warrant, interpretive argument, or procedural rationale. State assumptions and omitted proof obligations. |
| Complete worked case | Trace one case from initial question through setup, method, result, interpretation, and decision. Do not stop at producing a number. |
| Applications | What concrete task benefits? Explain the mapping from real task to method, why it is chosen, and how success is judged. Names of industries alone are insufficient. |
| Current/modern applications | What meaningful current uses, developments, or debates change today's decision? Verify dates and primary evidence; separate adoption, prototype, research claim, and speculation. |
| Assumptions and limitations | Which conditions support the claims? How can they fail? Separate inherent limitations, poor implementation, weak data, and misuse. |
| Detection and mitigation | How can a learner notice failure? Which mitigation addresses its mechanism, with what cost and remaining weakness? Include “no adequate mitigation” where true. |
| Alternatives and tradeoffs | What serious competing approaches or simpler baselines exist? Compare them on the same task, assumptions, resources, and success criteria. Explain when another choice wins. |
| Best practices | Which practices matter, why, when they apply, how to check them, and when the advice changes? Avoid context-free tips. |
| Misconceptions and boundaries | What plausible wrong rule produces a wrong decision? Supply discriminating cases and analogy limits. |
| Transfer and retention | Can the learner select, adapt, explain, and reject the method on a new case? Include unaided tasks, later reconstruction where in scope, and next-step routes. |

Completeness is evaluated across the course, not mechanically repeated on every slide. A narrowly scoped lesson can mark dimensions outside its promise with a reason. A full masterclass must not use narrow-lesson exemptions to evade the user's requested breadth.

## Weave the breadth into the reasoning

Start with purpose and a revealing use. Return to application after deriving the method. Introduce a limitation when the mechanism makes its cause visible. Introduce a mitigation as a response to that failure, then show its tradeoff. Introduce an alternative when it solves a remaining problem. Finish with a decision and transfer, not a pile of disconnected “advanced topics.”

Use a main route for the promised understanding and optional branches for specialized cases. A 100-plus-slide course is appropriate when the subject needs it. Chapters, pauses, worked examples, and navigation manage length; do not compress the central proof to preserve an arbitrary duration. Do not inflate length with redundant definitions.

## Limitation-to-decision chain

For each consequential limitation record:

1. Assumption or constraint.
2. Failure mechanism and concrete case.
3. Observable symptom or diagnostic; what that test can miss.
4. Mitigation and why it targets that mechanism.
5. Cost, tradeoff, and residual limitation.
6. Alternative or decision to avoid the method.
7. Transfer question that asks the learner to choose and justify a response.

A mitigation is not automatically a cure. Standardizing inputs, collecting more data, changing the objective, or adding automation can solve different problems and introduce others. For a historical or interpretive subject, mitigation may mean triangulating evidence, narrowing a claim, or exposing an unresolved dispute rather than fixing a mechanism.

## Applications with substance

An application record states context/date, actor or user, decision, inputs/evidence, method role, why it is suitable, outcome measure, failure case, alternative, and source. Use an accessible running case plus varied transfer contexts. Distinguish pedagogical toy examples from documented deployment and from proposed future uses.

For modern claims, inspect current primary papers, official technical documentation, standards, or original practitioners' evidence appropriate to the subject. Verify “widely used,” “state of the art,” or “industry standard” before using it. A company's announcement is evidence of its announcement, not independent proof of the benefit. Stable topics may need a current application rather than a recent change to the foundational theory.

## Fair alternatives and best practices

Compare strong conventional baselines as well as sophisticated methods. Use dimensions that matter to the learner: suitability, assumptions, interpretability, accuracy or evidential strength, data, compute, cost, latency, reliability, constraints, and maintenance where relevant. Do not invent benchmark numbers. A decision table should explain choices rather than crown one universal winner.

A practice has a rationale, trigger, implementation, verification, and exception. Where expert practice is contested, show the competing reasons and evidence rather than turning one source's preference into a rule.

## Adaptation across subject families

- Mathematics: definitions, invariants, proofs, necessary/sufficient conditions, constructive examples, limits, applications.
- Empirical science: mechanism/model, observation, experimental design, uncertainty, competing explanations, scale and validity.
- Engineering/software: system location, interface, failure modes, diagnosis, tradeoffs, implementation, operational practice.
- History/humanities: question, chronology/context, source provenance, competing interpretations, warrant, uncertainty and consequences. Do not invent a single settled causal proof.
- Practical craft: demonstrated technique, sensory/decision cues, practice with feedback, failure diagnosis, alternative techniques, transfer.
- Ethics/policy: competing values, stakeholders, consequences, constraints, strong opposing arguments, evidence versus normative judgment.

The same coverage questions can be useful across these subjects; the kind of evidence and explanation changes. Do not transplant PCA's sequence, diagram, calculus, or optimization objective.
